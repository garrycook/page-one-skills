# Page One: one page for every doctor, plus your prep sheet

Patients' records are spread across health systems that often can't see each other, so every new doctor starts from zero. A Page One fixes that: a single page, kept current, that the patient or a family caregiver hands to the doctor before the visit starts. What matters most is at the top, and every line shows where it came from. The format comes from The Page One Project (https://pageoneproject.org), a family project that uses it at real visits.

You make two things:
1. **Page One**, for the doctor. One printed page. Facts only.
2. **The prep sheet**, for the family. Questions, things to report, and chart corrections. It stays with them.

When there is more worth keeping than fits on one page, you also make **supporting pages** that go behind Page One (Step 3). Page One is still one page.

Everything is delivered as **one printout**, so the family prints once and sorts it into three sets: their prep sheet, a set for the front desk, and a set for the provider (Steps 3 and 5).

## Ground rules (never break these)

- **Facts only.** Page One does not diagnose and does not recommend treatment. You may point out facts a doctor would want to know (a lab value trending down for two years, a test that was ordered and never done, two offices listing different doses), but never say what the cause is or what should be done about it.
- **Every line has a source and a date** (for example "Clinic lab, Nov 4, 2026" or "Family report, Nov 2026"). If you can't source it, it doesn't go on the page.
- **Never invent or fill gaps.** If the records disagree, show both and mark it. If something is unknown, write "to confirm" and put it on the prep sheet. A wrong dose on a page a doctor trusts is worse than no page. Never work out a fact the person can simply tell you (a birth year from an age, a dose from a pill count): ask.
- **What the patient actually does beats what the chart says.** Ask the family. When they differ, Page One shows what is actually taken, and the prep sheet asks the office to fix its record.
- **A person checks every line before it's printed.** Say so plainly, and give them the checklist in Step 6.
- **Privacy.** This is someone's health information. Use only what the person gives you or connects, and don't repeat it anywhere else.
- This is not medical advice. If anything in the records suggests an emergency, tell the person to contact the doctor or call 911 instead of waiting for the visit.

## Step 1. Ask what you need

In one short message, ask:
1. Who the page is for, their full name and date of birth, and the person's relationship to them.
2. The visit: date, start time, doctor or clinic, the office address and phone, and the reason for the visit, if known. When a portal or an appointment notice is available, take these from it instead of from memory. (No visit yet? Make a general Page One for any doctor or an emergency.)
3. Where the records are. Use whatever they have:
   - Health records connected to this assistant (for example, through a health records connection, Apple Health, or Android Health Connect, where your app offers them). Use those first.
   - Downloaded visit summaries, after-visit summaries, lab reports, discharge papers, or a portal's "download my record" file. Ask them to upload or paste them.
   - Their own notes and memory, for things the records don't show.
   If their records are spread across portals they haven't set up yet, point them to https://pageoneproject.org/portals (or the page-one-portals skill) to find, link, and share them first.
4. What the family knows that the chart may not: which medicines are really being taken and how, anything that has run out, anything new since the last visit, and allergies.
5. Who makes decisions if the patient can't, with that person's phone number, and who else to call.

If the date of birth or the decision maker's phone number is still missing when you're ready to write, ask again before you finish. Don't print a blank line or a calculated date on a page a doctor will trust.

## Step 2. Read everything, then reconcile

**Start by checking what's new.** A summary goes stale in days. If an earlier Page One, patient summary, or records review exists, treat it as a starting point, and find out what has changed since its date before you reuse any line from it:
- If the patient portal is connected or open, read what's new since that date: test results, messages from the care team, the medication list (new, changed, or expired prescriptions), the visit list, and the upcoming appointment itself.
- Ask the person what has changed in what they actually take: anything that ran out, was refilled, started, stopped, or is taken differently now.
- If you can't get into the portal (signed out, not connected), say so. Ask the person to sign in, or to tell you what the new items say. Don't present old facts as current.
- Date each line by when it was last confirmed. A line carried over from the old summary keeps the old date; a line checked today gets today's date.
- If a notice says something new was posted and you can't find it, say exactly where you looked instead of guessing what it was.

**Trace every refill that hasn't arrived.** When a medicine has run out, or the person is waiting on one, follow it through the records before you write the line:
- Was a refill requested, and how did the office answer? A reply such as "you should have enough" means nothing was sent, even if the person believes it's on the way.
- Does a current prescription exist? Note who wrote it, when, how many refills are left, and where it was sent (local pharmacy or mail order).
- When one medicine is taken as two strengths or comes from two offices, check each piece separately. One office may cover only part of the dose.
- Put what you find on Page One as facts ("Out since about [date]. Refill requested [date]; office replied [date] that a supply was sent [date]. No new prescription found."), and put the ask on the prep sheet ("Can you send a short supply to my local pharmacy today?").

Then, before writing, go through all of it and build working notes (these are for you, not the page):
- **Medications:** one row per medicine: what each health system's record says, and what the patient actually takes. Flag mismatches, expired prescriptions still being taken, medicines on a list that were stopped, and medicines the patient is out of.
- **Problems:** active conditions, with when each started. Note anything in imaging or notes that never made it onto the problem list.
- **Open loops:** tests or referrals that were ordered and never done, results not yet back, and follow-ups recommended and never scheduled. These are often the most useful things on the page.
- **Trends:** labs and vitals over time (oldest to newest), especially ones out of range or drifting.
- **Recent care:** hospital stays, emergency visits, procedures, and new diagnoses in the last year.
- **Conflicts:** anywhere two sources disagree.

## Step 3. Write Page One

Page One is one printed page (US Letter), always. It is what the doctor reads in the room, in the few minutes there are, and it has to stand alone. Hard limits: at most 5 items under "Needs attention now", at most 3 under "Waiting on", and one line per medication. Never shrink the type to cram more in.

Use this layout, in this order:

```
PAGE ONE: [Patient full name]        Date of birth: [date]        Updated [date]
Visit: [Day, date, start time]. [Doctor or clinic], [office address], [office phone].
Prepared by [name], [relationship], from [sources]. Facts only. No diagnosis, no recommendations.

NEEDS ATTENTION NOW
1. [The fact, in one or two plain sentences, with numbers and dates]   [Source, date]
2. ...

WAITING ON
- [Pending result, open order, or referral, and when it was ordered]   [Source, date]

ACTIVE PROBLEMS
- [Condition] (since [year])   [Source]

MEDICATIONS (what is actually taken)
- [Name] [dose], [how often]   [Source: pharmacy fill / record / family, date]
  [If a record differs: "Record at [system] says [X]."]
  [If the patient is out: "OUT since [date]."]

ALLERGIES
- [Allergen]: [reaction]   [Source]

RECENT CARE (last 12 months)
- [Date]: [Hospital stay / ER visit / procedure], [where]

WHO TO CALL
- [Name], [relationship]: makes decisions if the patient cannot. [Phone]
- Primary care: [clinic], [phone]
- Other doctors: [specialty, clinic]

[Set label: see "One printout, three sets" below]
Format by The Page One Project, pageoneproject.org        Page 1 of [N]        Version: [date and time made]
```

The Visit line goes on every Page One made for a specific appointment, and the same line heads the prep sheet, so nobody has to look up where and when. Give one time only: the time the visit starts. Don't print a separate "arrive by" time next to it; two times on a page invite showing up at the wrong one. For a general Page One with no visit, leave the Visit line out.

The version stamp (date and time the page was made) goes in the footer of Page One, of every supporting page, and of the prep sheet, and changes every time one is remade. Pages get reprinted as facts come in, and the stamp is how the family tells the current copy from an old one.

Choosing "Needs attention now": what a doctor seeing this patient today would most regret not knowing. Good examples: a dangerous mismatch between records, a medicine the patient has run out of, a test ordered long ago and never done, a value drifting the wrong way, a recent hospital stay. Put the most important first.

### When it doesn't fit: move it back, don't throw it away

A true, sourced fact that doesn't fit on Page One moves to a supporting page for the chart. Move things back in this order, and stop as soon as Page One fits:
1. Visits and hospital stays more than 12 months old.
2. Other doctors and suppliers under "Who to call" (keep the decision maker and primary care).
3. Detail on as-needed medicines and supplements (fold each group into one line on Page One).
4. Background inside an item: keep the fact and the latest numbers, move the history.
5. Resolved or long-stable problems.
6. Family history, social history, and outdated lists still on file at other offices.

These never leave Page One: every "Needs attention now" item, allergies, the "what is actually taken" medication lines, any dose that differs between records, anything the patient is out of, the decision maker and phone, and the source and date on each line.

### Supporting pages (optional)

There are two readers. The doctor in the room has minutes, and reads Page One. The chart keeps whatever is handed in, and anyone can read it later; supporting pages are mostly for that reader, and they are within reach if the doctor wants the detail. Make them only when there is material that didn't fit and is worth keeping. Many visits need none.

- They follow Page One as pages 2, 3 and so on of the same document. Head each one: "[Patient full name], date of birth [date]. SUPPORTING DETAIL, page [n] of [N]. Page 1 is the summary." Don't label them "for the chart": Page One goes into the chart too. Use the same footer and version stamp as Page One.
- Good contents: the full medication comparison (one row per medicine, a column for each office's record, and a column for what is actually taken); lab and vital sign trends as tables; family and social history; older visits and hospital stays; records that live outside the portal; outdated lists still on file elsewhere; and a "Corrections requested" list written as facts ("Record says X. Patient takes Y.").
- Same ground rules: facts only, with a source and date on every line.
- Page One never depends on them. Don't write "see page 3" for anything the doctor needs today. If it matters today, it's on Page One.
- Keep them few. One or two is usual, and more than three is a sign that something should be cut instead of moved. Every added page is one more thing the family has to check before printing.

### One printout, three sets

Deliver one file, in the most printable form your tools allow (a PDF if you can make one; otherwise clean text the person can paste into a document). The family prints it once, single-sided, and sorts it into three sets. Put the pages in this order, and print a set label in the footer of every page, so nothing ends up in the wrong hands:

1. **The prep sheet** (Step 4). Footer: "MY COPY: prep sheet. Not for the front desk or the provider."
2. **Set 1, front desk:** Page One plus any supporting pages. Footer on every page: "SET 1 of 2, FRONT DESK: hand in all [N] pages of this set to be scanned into the chart."
3. **Set 2, provider:** the same pages again. Footer on Page One: "SET 2 of 2, PROVIDER: hand this page to the provider. Keep pages 2 to [N] ready in case they are wanted." Footer on each supporting page: "SET 2 of 2, PROVIDER: keep this page ready in case the provider wants it."

Number the pages within each set ("Page 1 of 3"), not across the whole printout. When there are no supporting pages, each set is Page One by itself and the labels shorten to match. Say "print single-sided" wherever you give printing instructions: double-sided printing puts one set on the back of another.

If you can only produce text, give the three sets in the same order under clear headings.

**Hospital stays:** if the person has a small thermal printer (the "4-inch strip" kind), also offer a one-column version of Page One, so they can tear off a fresh copy for each new nurse or doctor. Supporting pages don't go on the strip.

## Step 4. Write the prep sheet (the family keeps this one)

```
MY COPY. NOT FOR THE DOCTOR. Keep these sheets.
VISIT PREP: [Patient]
Visit: [Day, date, start time]. [Doctor or clinic], [office address], [office phone].

How to sort this printout ([total] sheets, printed single-sided)
- First sheets: this prep sheet. Keep it.
- Next [N] sheets, SET 1, FRONT DESK: hand them all in at check-in and ask for the set to be scanned into the chart.
- Last [N] sheets, SET 2, PROVIDER: hand Page One to the provider as they walk in. Keep the rest ready in case they're wanted.

What we want from this visit
- [One or two sentences]

Before you go
[ ] Online check-in, if the portal offers it
[ ] Insurance card and photo ID (say so if the portal or a notice shows an insurance or billing problem)
[ ] The printout, sorted into its three sets and paper-clipped (don't staple the front desk set)
[ ] The actual bottles for anything in question (over-the-counter products, supplements, a dose the records disagree on)
[ ] Any outside record this office doesn't have (two copies: one to scan, one for the doctor)
[ ] A pen

Questions (most important first)
1. [Specific question in the patient's own voice, naming the fact behind it]
2. ...

Since the last visit
- [Symptoms, changes, side effects, falls, ER visits, new medicines, medicines that ran out, how a new medicine is going]

Ask them to fix the record
[ ] [Each wrong dose, stopped medicine still listed, missing allergy, outdated history]

Notes (leave room to write during the visit)

Version: [date and time made]
```

The banner at the top is there so the prep sheet doesn't get handed over or scanned by mistake. Keep "Before you go" to what this visit actually needs; drop lines that don't apply.

Good questions are specific and tied to the records ("My blood sugar has gone up on each of the last three tests. Should anything change, and can we recheck it today?"), not general ("Is my blood sugar okay?"). Aim for 5 to 10.

## Step 5. How to use them (tell the person)

- Print the whole file once, single-sided, and sort it by the label at the bottom of each sheet.
- Set 1 goes to the front desk at check-in, all pages, with a request to scan it into the chart.
- From Set 2, hand Page One to the doctor as they walk in, before the visit starts. Keep the rest of Set 2 on the desk or in hand, in case they want the detail.
- The prep sheet stays with the family. Bring a pen.
- If a page was remade, check the version stamp and throw out the older copies before leaving the house.
- After the visit, update Page One with anything new, so the next visit starts from today.

## Step 6. Review checklist (before anything is printed)

Give the person this list and ask them to check each item:
- [ ] Every medication matches the bottles at home (name, strength, how often).
- [ ] Every line has a source and date, and each one is right.
- [ ] The name and date of birth are right.
- [ ] Nothing is on any page that the patient wouldn't want this doctor to see.
- [ ] "Who to call" is current, the phone number is right, and the decision maker knows they're listed.
- [ ] Anything marked "to confirm" is either confirmed or moved to the prep sheet.
- [ ] The visit date, start time, and office address match the appointment notice.
- [ ] Page One stands alone: nothing the doctor needs today is only on a supporting page.
- [ ] Every sheet carries the right set label, and the prep sheet is marked as yours.
- [ ] These are the newest copies (check the version stamp).
